Remember where you were when Brexit happened? Polls said Remain. Markets said Remain. Pundits said Remain. Then Leave won with 52% of the vote.

Or November 2016. Prediction markets gave Hillary Clinton an 85% chance on election night. FiveThirtyEight was more cautious at 71%. Donald Trump won the Electoral College. The "impossible" happened — again.

These weren't flukes. They were symptoms of a fundamentally broken system for forecasting the future.

## The Problem: Markets Reflect Money, Not Accuracy

Prediction markets — Polymarket, Kalshi, PredictIt before them — have a seductive pitch: let people put skin in the game, and the "wisdom of crowds" will produce accurate forecasts. Money talks. Truth wins.

Except it doesn't. Not reliably.

Prediction markets are information aggregation mechanisms, yes. But what they aggregate is **where capital is concentrated**, not where the truth is. A whale with $100,000 to spend can move a market more than 100 calibrated forecasters with no money. The signal gets distorted by wealth, not sharpened by it.

Research from the Good Judgment Project — run by Philip Tetlock and Barbara Mellers at the University of Pennsylvania — demonstrated something striking: a relatively small group of carefully selected, scored forecasters consistently **outperformed prediction markets** on geopolitical questions. Not sometimes. Consistently. By significant margins.

These weren't people betting money. They were people betting their *reputation*. Their scores. Their track records.

That's the key insight: **accuracy improves when you measure accuracy, not when you wager capital**.

## Why Markets Get It Wrong

Markets fail at prediction for several structural reasons:

**1. Wealth asymmetry.** Markets weight participants by bankroll, not by skill. A wealthy but wrong participant has more influence than a penniless genius.

**2. Manipulation vulnerability.** We've seen repeated examples of markets being deliberately moved — sometimes for political purposes, sometimes for profit on related positions. When Meta's internal researchers studied their own prediction markets, they found that a few large traders consistently moved prices away from the base rate.

**Thin liquidity on important questions.** The most consequential questions — geopolitical events, regime changes, black swans — are often the ones with the thinnest markets. Few people want to tie up capital for months on a 5% probability event.

**4. No scoring over time.** Markets give you a price at a moment. They don't tell you *who* was right and *why*. There's no accountability, no track record, no learning loop.

**5. Regulatory constraints.** In most of the world, prediction markets face severe legal restrictions. The EU, UK, and many other jurisdictions treat them as gambling. This limits participation to a narrow, self-selected group — typically young, male, and financially comfortable. Not exactly a representative sample of human judgment.

## The Solution: Prediction Polling + Blind Consensus

Prediction polling takes a different approach. Instead of asking "what are you willing to bet?" it asks "what do you actually think will happen?" — and then scores the answer.

The foundation is decades old. The **Delphi method**, developed at RAND Corporation in the 1950s, works on a simple principle:

1. Ask experts for their estimates **independently** (no group discussion).
2. Aggregate the results anonymously.
3. Share the aggregate with the group.
4. Repeat.

This process — blind independent judgment, followed by aggregation — consistently outperforms face-to-face expert panels. Why? Because it eliminates **anchoring** (latching onto the first opinion stated), **groupthink** (conforming to the majority), and **authority bias** (deferring to the loudest voice in the room).

Tetlock's research took this further. His "superforecasters" — the top 2% of participants in the Good Judgment tournament — weren't subject matter experts. They were generalists who were good at updating their beliefs, thinking in probabilities, and staying intellectually honest. And as a group, they beat intelligence analysts with classified information. They beat prediction markets. They beat the wisdom-of-crowds baseline.

The secret wasn't genius. It was **process**: independent forecasts, scored with the Brier score, aggregated without bias.

## Why You Should Care

We live in an era of radical uncertainty. AI is reshaping industries. Geopolitics is more volatile than at any point since the Cold War. Climate events are accelerating. The pace of change is staggering.

In this environment, the ability to make good forecasts — individually and collectively — isn't an academic exercise. It's a survival skill. Organizations that can accurately assess probabilities make better decisions. Individuals who think in probabilities are less likely to be surprised.

Prediction markets gave us a glimpse of what collective intelligence could look like. But they confused the mechanism (markets) with the goal (accuracy). Prediction polling dispenses with the mechanism and focuses on the goal.

The Delphi method proved it works. Tetlock's superforecasters proved it scales. Brier scoring proved it's measurable.
