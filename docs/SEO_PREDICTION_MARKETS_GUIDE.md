# What Are Prediction Markets? The Complete Guide for 2026

In September 2024, Polymarket's odds on the U.S. presidential election stood at roughly 55% for Donald Trump — a figure that seemed bold at the time but proved remarkably accurate compared to traditional polling aggregates that showed a near dead heat. That single prediction, visible to anyone with an internet connection, crystallized what researchers have known for decades: **prediction markets** can aggregate information in ways that polls, pundits, and even intelligence agencies sometimes cannot.

But what exactly are prediction markets, and why should you care? Whether you've heard the term on a news broadcast or stumbled across it on social media, this guide explains everything you need to know about how prediction markets work, their history, their accuracy, and the platforms shaping this rapidly growing industry in 2026.

## How Prediction Markets Work

At its core, a **prediction market** is a trading platform where people buy and sell contracts based on the likelihood of future events. The price of a contract directly reflects the market's collective estimate of that event's probability.

Here's the basic mechanism: if a contract pays out $1 if an event occurs and $0 if it doesn't, and the contract is currently trading at $0.72, the market is pricing that event at a 72% probability. This elegant relationship — **price equals probability** — is what makes prediction markets so powerful.

Participants who believe the true probability is higher than the market price buy "Yes" shares. Those who believe it's lower buy "No" shares (or sell Yes shares). When the event resolves, correct positions pay out and incorrect ones expire worthless. The profit incentive drives participants to research, analyze, and incorporate information that might not yet be reflected in the price.

For example, a contract asking "Will the Federal Reserve cut interest rates by July 2026?" might trade at $0.35 early in the year. If incoming economic data shifts expectations, the price moves — sometimes within minutes. This real-time updating is what distinguishes prediction markets from static polls.

## A Brief History of Prediction Markets

Prediction markets have deeper academic roots than most people realize. The concept dates back to the **Iowa Electronic Markets (IEM)**, launched in 1988 by researchers at the University of Iowa. The IEM allowed participants to trade contracts on U.S. presidential elections, and it quickly demonstrated that market prices consistently outperformed traditional polls — a finding that has held up across decades of research.

The early 2000s saw the rise of **Intrade**, a popular real-money prediction market that attracted mainstream media attention for its election forecasting. Intrade's contracts covered everything from politics to box office results, and its accuracy during the 2008 and 2012 elections made it a fixture in political coverage. However, regulatory pressure forced Intrade to shut down U.S. operations in 2013.

**Betfair**, founded in 2000, took a different path. Based in the UK and operating under a gambling license, Betfair became the world's largest betting exchange and proved that prediction market mechanics could scale to millions of users and billions in volume.

The modern era is defined by **Polymarket**, which launched in 2020 and leveraged crypto infrastructure to bypass traditional regulatory constraints. By 2024, Polymarket had become a household name during the U.S. elections. Its trading volume exceeded $3 billion that year alone, and by 2026 the broader prediction market industry has surpassed $150 billion in cumulative volume.

## Prediction Markets vs. Polls vs. Experts

One of the most frequently asked questions is simple: **are prediction markets really more accurate than polls?**

The academic evidence is compelling. A landmark study by Berg, Nelson, and Rietz (2008) analyzing the Iowa Electronic Markets found that market prices were more accurate than polls in 74% of the 96 elections studied. Philip Tetlock's research on the Good Judgment Project — a tournament funded by U.S. intelligence agencies — demonstrated that structured forecasting approaches outperformed intelligence analysts with access to classified information by 30% or more.

The key advantage of prediction markets is their ability to **aggregate diverse information in real time**. A poll captures a snapshot of opinion at a single moment. An expert provides a single judgment. A prediction market continuously updates as thousands of participants — each with different information, models, and incentives — trade on their beliefs.

Markets are also self-correcting. When new information emerges, participants who recognize its significance immediately trade on it, adjusting the price. Polls can't do this between survey waves, and individual experts may be slow to update their priors.

That said, prediction markets aren't perfect. They can be manipulated in low-liquidity environments, they can reflect the demographics of their user base (which skews young, male, crypto-literate, and affluent), and they can exhibit herd behavior during periods of extreme uncertainty. The 2024 U.S. election cycle showed that large capital flows from a small number of accounts could move prices independently of genuine information — a phenomenon Bloomberg documented extensively.

The honest answer: prediction markets are one powerful tool among many. They tend to outperform any single alternative, but they're strongest when combined with other forecasting methods.

## Types of Prediction Markets

Not all prediction markets work the same way. The main types include:

**Binary markets** are the simplest and most common. They ask a yes/no question — "Will X happen by date Y?" — and contracts pay out $1 if the event occurs and $0 if it doesn't. Polymarket and Kalshi primarily use this format.

**Categorical markets** ask "Which of these outcomes will occur?" with multiple mutually exclusive options. A market might ask "Who will win the next election?" with contracts for each candidate. The prices across all options sum to roughly $1.

**Scalar (or continuous) markets** ask for a numerical value — "What will the unemployment rate be in January 2027?" — and contracts pay out proportionally based on how close the final number is to the predicted range.

**Combinatorial markets** allow participants to bet on combinations of outcomes — for example, conditional predictions like "If candidate A wins the primary, will they win the general election?" These are more complex but capture richer information.

## The Science of Scoring: Brier Scores and Proper Scoring Rules

How do we know if a prediction was "good"? This is where **proper scoring rules** come in — the mathematical foundation that separates serious forecasting from guessing.

The most widely used scoring rule is the **Brier score**, developed by Glenn Brier in 1950. For a binary event, the Brier score is calculated as (forecast − outcome)², where the outcome is 1 if the event happened and 0 if it didn't. A perfect forecast scores 0, and a completely wrong forecast (assigning 1.0 to an event that doesn't happen) scores 1. The lower the score, the better.

Crucially, Brier scores are **proper** — meaning the mathematically optimal strategy is to report your true belief. You can't game the system by exaggerating or hedging. This property is what makes proper scoring rules so valuable for forecasting platforms.

**Logarithmic scoring** is another proper scoring rule that penalizes confident wrong answers more heavily. It's the standard used by Metaculus and is also implemented on Baycast. Both scoring methods reward calibration: assigning probabilities that match the actual frequency of events across many predictions.

Over time, these scores build a **track record** — a forecaster's resume that shows not just whether they were right, but how well-calibrated their probability estimates were across dozens or hundreds of questions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are prediction markets legal?

It depends on your jurisdiction and the platform. Kalshi is fully regulated by the CFTC in the United States. Polymarket operates in a regulatory gray area using crypto infrastructure. Prediction polling platforms like Baycast are not markets and avoid gambling classification entirely. Always check your local laws before participating.

### Are prediction markets gambling?

Prediction markets share mechanical similarities with gambling — you risk money on uncertain outcomes. However, their primary purpose is information aggregation, not entertainment. The academic community generally treats them as distinct from gambling because they serve a truth-seeking function. That said, some regulators (particularly in Nevada and several US states) disagree.

### How accurate are prediction markets?

Research shows prediction markets outperform polls and individual experts in about 70-75% of cases for political events. Their accuracy depends on liquidity (more participants = better estimates) and the clarity of the resolution criteria. The Iowa Electronic Markets have demonstrated this advantage consistently since 1988.

### What's the difference between a prediction market and a prediction poll?

A prediction market involves buying and selling contracts with financial stakes. A prediction poll asks participants to submit probability estimates without financial risk. Polls scored with proper scoring rules like the Brier score can produce equally accurate forecasts while being more accessible. [Learn more about prediction polling](/docs/WHY_PREDICTION_POLLS.md).

### Can you make money on prediction markets?

Yes — profitable traders exist on every platform. However, a Wall Street Journal investigation found that on Polymarket, profits are concentrated among a small number of sophisticated accounts while the average retail user loses money. Treat prediction markets as a serious intellectual exercise, not a get-rich-quick scheme.

### What is a Brier score?

A Brier score measures the accuracy of probabilistic predictions. For each forecast, it calculates the squared difference between your predicted probability and the actual outcome (0 or 1). Scores range from 0 (perfect) to 1 (worst). It's a proper scoring rule, meaning you can't game it — your best strategy is always to report your true belief.

### How do prediction markets handle manipulation?

Markets with deep liquidity are resistant to manipulation — if someone tries to move the price with a large bet, other participants who recognize the mispricing will trade against them, restoring accuracy. Low-liquidity markets are more vulnerable. The 2024 election cycle showed that capital concentration can distort prices even on high-profile events.

### What is the Blind Consensus Protocol?

Baycast's blind consensus protocol is a two-phase forecasting method inspired by the Delphi method. In Phase A, participants submit forecasts without seeing others' predictions (eliminating anchoring bias). In Phase B, participants see the aggregate and can optionally revise. This produces more accurate forecasts than single-round polling or transparent market pricing.
