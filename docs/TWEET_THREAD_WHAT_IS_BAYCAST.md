---

## Tweet 2/12

The problem: every prediction platform (Polymarket, Metaculus, Manifold) shows you the crowd's answer BEFORE you predict.

This seems harmless. It isn't.

When you see the odds first, your estimate shifts 10-20% toward the consensus. That's not wisdom — that's anchoring.

---

## Tweet 3/12

This is called the herding problem, and it's well-documented in behavioral economics.

The "wisdom of crowds" only works when each person brings INDEPENDENT information. A doctor knows healthcare. A trader knows markets. The magic is in combining those independent views.

---

## Tweet 4/12

The moment you show people the crowd's answer before they commit, that independence collapses.

Everyone converges. The unique signal each person carried — the very thing that made the crowd smart — gets washed out. You don't get a wise crowd. You get a herd.

---

## Tweet 5/12

So we built Baycast around a different principle: predict FIRST, see the crowd AFTER.

It's called the Blind Consensus Protocol — inspired by the Delphi method from the 1950s and validated by the IARPA-funded Good Judgment Project, which outperformed prediction markets by 25%.

---

## Tweet 6/12

Here's how it works:

1️⃣ A question goes live (binary, clear criteria, hard deadline)
2️⃣ Everyone submits a probability estimate WITHOUT seeing others
3️⃣ After the blind phase, all forecasts are revealed
4️⃣ One revision allowed with full context

Both phases scored.

---

## Tweet 7/12

Scoring uses Brier scores — a proper scoring rule where honesty is mathematically optimal.

Not "be right relative to the market price" (prediction markets).
Be right relative to REALITY.

Your best strategy is always to report your true belief. Nothing else.

---

## Tweet 8/12

This creates something unique: two separate skill measurements for every forecaster.

🔵 Independent Judgment — how accurate are you before seeing anyone else?
🟢 Information Integration — how well do you update when you learn from the crowd?

Different skills. Both trackable.

A Turing Test for forecasting — not conversation, but actual prediction accuracy.
