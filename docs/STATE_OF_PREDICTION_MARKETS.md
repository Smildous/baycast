# The State of Prediction Markets in 2026

## How Crowdsourced Forecasting Went From Niche Experiment to a $150 Billion Industry — and Where It's Heading Next

*Published: May 2026 · Baycast Research*

---

In 2024, prediction markets were a curiosity — a political parlor trick that occasionally made headlines during election season. By mid-2026, they have become a $150+ billion industry, attracted valuations in the tens of billions, and forced regulators, media organizations, and Wall Street to reckon with a fundamental question: **can a crowd of strangers predict the future better than experts?**

The answer, increasingly, is yes — with important caveats.

---

## The Numbers: A Market That Grew 4x in a Single Year

The growth statistics are staggering. According to CertiK's 2025 year-end report, prediction market trading volumes grew **4x to reach $63.5 billion** in 2025 alone. By early 2026, cumulative trading volume across all platforms surpassed **$150 billion**, per data from CryptoRank and MEXC. Monthly volumes grew 130x from 2024 baselines, according to on-chain analytics from Keyrock and Dune.

Bernstein, the investment research firm, now estimates that prediction markets could reach **$1 trillion in annual volume by 2030** — a forecast that, in this industry, seems almost conservative given the trajectory.

The two dominant platforms — **Kalshi** and **Polymarket** — are each reportedly exploring fundraising rounds at **$20 billion valuations**, double their previous rounds according to the Wall Street Journal. Polymarket, the crypto-native leader, has reportedly been targeting a $400 million funding round at a $15 billion valuation. Gemini, the Winklevoss-owned exchange, has secured a derivatives license to enter the space as a challenger.

The duopoly is real, but it's not going unchallenged. New entrants like XO Market and Myriad are betting on user-generated markets, while Manifold Markets continues to serve the "play money" forecasting community.

---

## Platform Breakdown: The Big Four

### Polymarket — The Crypto Native Giant

Built on the Polygon blockchain and using USDC for settlements, Polymarket has become the default platform for political and cultural predictions. Its 2024 election markets drew millions of users and billions in volume. In 2025, the platform expanded into sports, science, and geopolitical event contracts.

Polymarket operates outside the traditional US regulatory framework by leveraging crypto infrastructure, though it has faced increasing scrutiny. A WSJ investigation found that the average retail user loses money, with profits concentrated among a small number of sophisticated traders ("sharks"). The platform has countered that it provides transparent, real-time probability estimates that outperform traditional polling.

### Kalshi — The Regulated Incumbent

Kalshi is the only CFTC-regulated prediction market in the US, giving it a legitimacy advantage for institutional users and those who prefer dollar-denominated, fiat-backed contracts. Its legal battle with the CFTC over event contracts — particularly around sports and politics — has shaped the regulatory landscape.

Kalshi's volumes surged alongside Polymarket's in 2025, and its push into sports contracts (despite NFL opposition) has been a major growth driver. A legal fight with the state of Nevada over its operations is ongoing.

### Metaculus — The Forecaster's Platform

Metaculus occupies a unique niche: it's a community of serious forecasters who compete on accuracy rather than profit. Its annual ACX/Metaculus forecasting contest attracts top-tier participants. The platform uses median community predictions rather than market prices, and its track record on geopolitical and scientific questions has been impressive.

Research from the Good Judgment Project and subsequent academic work has consistently shown that trained forecasters — particularly "superforecasters" — outperform intelligence analysts with access to classified information. Metaculus is where this community lives.

### Manifold Markets — The Play Money Sandbox

Manifold uses play money (mana) rather than real currency, which removes gambling concerns but also reduces the skin-in-the-game incentive that makes prediction markets accurate. Despite this, Manifold has become a valuable tool for quick forecasting and hypothesis testing, particularly in the AI safety and effective altruism communities.

## The Regulatory Landscape: A Tug-of-War in Washington

Prediction markets sit at the uncomfortable intersection of financial regulation and gambling law, and 2025-2026 has seen regulators and Congress grapple with how to handle them.

**The CFTC** has been the primary federal regulator, with Chairman Michael Selig confirmed in 2026 and six key issues to watch. The agency's approach has oscillated between accommodation and enforcement, particularly around event contracts that some argue cross into sports betting.

**Congress** has taken notice. CNBC reported that Kalshi and Polymarket are actively lobbying as Congressional committees examine insider trading rules and whether prediction markets constitute gambling. The NFL has formally expressed concern to Congress about the rise of prediction markets intersecting with sports betting.

**Nevada** has initiated a protracted legal fight against Kalshi, arguing that the platform violates state gambling laws. The outcome could set a precedent for how states interact with federally regulated prediction markets.

The fundamental regulatory question remains unresolved: **Are prediction markets financial instruments, gambling, or something new?** The answer will determine whether the industry continues its explosive growth or faces a regulatory crackdown.

## Accuracy: Do Crowds Really Know Best?

The empirical evidence is increasingly compelling:

- **Political forecasting**: Polymarket's odds during the 2024 US elections consistently outperformed traditional polling aggregates. The platform's real-time pricing mechanism captured late-breaking shifts that polls missed.
- **Superforecasters**: Research from the Good Judgment Project (originally funded by IARPA) demonstrated that top forecasters can outperform intelligence analysts with access to classified information by 30%+ on geopolitical questions.
- **Market efficiency**: A Forbes analysis titled "The Polymarket Effect" documented how prediction markets are beating traditional expert forecasts across multiple domains.
- **Caveats**: The WSJ's finding that most retail traders lose money raises questions about whether market accuracy comes from the wisdom of crowds or the dominance of a few informed actors. Additionally, markets can be manipulated — particularly in lower-volume contracts — and can exhibit herd behavior during periods of high uncertainty.

The current US-Iran conflict is a real-time test case. Prediction markets have been remarkably volatile on ceasefire probabilities, reflecting genuine uncertainty rather than a failure of the mechanism. The honest answer is sometimes "we don't know" — and prediction markets are uniquely good at expressing that through 50/50 odds.

## Where Baycast Fits: Prediction Polling, Not Gambling

This is where **Baycast** enters the picture — and it enters with a deliberately different philosophy.

The existing prediction market landscape splits into two camps: regulated gambling (Kalshi) and crypto-native betting (Polymarket). Both require users to risk real money, creating barriers to entry (geographic restrictions, KYC requirements, financial risk) that limit participation to a relatively small, financially-motivated crowd.

**Baycast occupies a third position: prediction polling.**

Rather than asking users to bet money on outcomes, Baycast asks them to weigh in with their probability estimates — creating a dataset that reflects broad sentiment from a diverse participant base. This approach has several advantages:

1. **Accessibility**: No financial barriers, no KYC, no geographic restrictions. Anyone with an opinion can participate.
2. **Diversity of participants**: Financial prediction markets skew toward economically privileged participants. Prediction polling can capture insights from a broader demographic cross-section.
3. **Less noise, more signal**: Without financial incentives to manipulate or game the system, Baycast's predictions may be less "efficient" in the narrow market sense but potentially more representative of genuine collective intelligence.
4. **Complementary to markets**: Baycast's data can serve as a calibration tool — comparing what the crowd believes (polling) against what the market prices (trading). Discrepancies between the two are themselves informative.

The superforecasting community has long known that the best predictions come from diverse, independent estimates combined thoughtfully. Baycast is building the infrastructure to do that at scale.

## What's Next: 2026 and Beyond

Several trends will shape the prediction market ecosystem in the coming months:

1. **ETF expansion**: The SEC's approval of BTC and ETH spot ETFs has opened the door for SOL, XRP, and other crypto ETFs. This regulatory momentum could extend to prediction market-related financial products.

2. **Sports integration**: Despite NFL opposition, the convergence of prediction markets and sports betting is accelerating. Gemini's entry with a derivatives license signals institutional interest.

3. **AI forecasting**: As AI models improve, the question of whether machines can outperform human forecasters will become testable at scale. Early evidence suggests humans still have an edge in novel, ambiguous situations.

4. **Geopolitical volatility**: The Iran conflict, China-US tensions, and tariff uncertainty have made geopolitical forecasting more valuable — and more difficult — than ever.

5. **Mainstream adoption**: As volumes approach $1 trillion by decade's end (per Bernstein), prediction markets will become a standard input for decision-making in media, business, and government.

---

## Conclusion

Prediction markets have come a long way from their academic origins. In 2026, they are a legitimate industry, a valuable forecasting tool, and a subject of intense regulatory debate. The key insight — that aggregated individual predictions can outperform individual experts — has been validated repeatedly.

But the industry's growth has also exposed real problems: retail traders losing money, regulatory uncertainty, and the risk of markets being gamed by sophisticated actors. The path forward requires thoughtful design, appropriate regulation, and new approaches that expand participation beyond the financially motivated minority.

Baycast's bet is simple: **the best predictions come from the broadest possible crowd, not just the best-funded.** In a world drowning in punditry and noise, that's a proposition worth testing.
