## The Gambling Problem Nobody Wants to Name

Here's what the prediction market industry won't tell you: **they're not forecasting platforms. They're casinos with better branding.**

When you put money on the line, the signal degrades. It's not a controversial claim — it's basic behavioral economics. People bet on what they *want* to happen, not what they *think* will happen. An OpenAI employee was recently fired for insider trading on prediction markets. The Senate literally banned its own members from participating. Romania banned 300 prediction sites and created a €5M treatment fund for gambling addiction. CNN is investigating "death markets." Politico reports that "alleged prediction market violations start stacking up."

The $15 billion valuation of this industry rests on a fundamental contradiction: the platforms claim to produce collective wisdom, but their financial incentives actively undermine it.

## The Herding Problem Nobody Notices

Even if you strip away the money, there's a deeper problem that almost every platform shares: **they show you the crowd's answer before you give yours.**

Polymarket shows you the price. Metaculus shows you the median. Manifold shows you the odds. Kalshi shows you the market. You think you're making an independent forecast. You're actually anchoring to the crowd.

Research consistently shows that exposure to a group estimate shifts your answer by 10-20% toward the consensus. The "wisdom of crowds" effect — the entire theoretical justification for these platforms — requires *independent* judgment. The moment you see the odds, that independence collapses. You're not adding signal to the system. You're adding an echo.

This is the problem with the current landscape, and it's the problem that no amount of regulation will fix. McCormick and Gillibrand's bill addresses market manipulation and investor protection. But it doesn't address the fact that the core mechanism — visible, tradeable aggregates — structurally encourages herding.

## A Better Approach: Scored Prediction Polling

We built Baycast because we think the prediction industry needs to take its own methodology seriously.

Baycast is a scored prediction polling platform. You predict outcomes on real-world questions — tech, geopolitics, science, finance — and we measure your accuracy over time using Brier scores. No money changes hands. No crypto wallets. No regulatory gray zones.

Two things make this approach different from everything else in the space:

**First: the Blind Consensus Protocol.** When you answer a question on Baycast, you do it *without seeing what anyone else predicted*. Only after you've committed your answer does the crowd aggregate get revealed. You can then revise your prediction with full context — but your blind answer is preserved and scored independently.

This isn't a new idea. It's the Delphi method, developed by the RAND Corporation in the 1950s and refined over 70+ years of research. The IARPA-funded Good Judgment Project used methods very similar to this and **outperformed prediction markets by 25%**. Not a little. A quarter better. Using a method older than the internet.

The intelligence community knows this works. We rebuilt it for the web.

**Second: proper scoring without financial stakes.** Brier scores are proper scoring rules, meaning that honest probability reporting is mathematically optimal. You can't game them by hedging or exaggerating. They decompose into calibration, discrimination, and uncertainty components — so we can tell you not just *whether* you're accurate, but *why*.

Your track record reflects your judgment. Not your bankroll. Not your willingness to lose $1,000 on a long shot. Just how well you understand the world.
