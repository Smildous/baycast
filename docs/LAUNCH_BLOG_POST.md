## The Prediction Market Trap

Prediction markets just raised at a $15 billion valuation. They've been called "the most accurate forecasting tool ever built." Congress cites them. Journalists quote their odds. And the entire industry is built on a lie — not the kind you can prove wrong with data, but the kind that's so baked into the assumptions that nobody even questions it.

Here's the lie: *showing people what the crowd thinks before they answer makes the crowd smarter.*

It doesn't. It makes the crowd a herd.

There's a well-documented finding in behavioral economics: when you ask people to estimate something *before* they see what others think, the aggregate is significantly more accurate than when you let them see the crowd first. Research shows that exposure to the group estimate shifts your answer by 10–20% toward the consensus. That's not wisdom of crowds. That's anchoring. And it systematically destroys the independent signal that makes collective intelligence work in the first place.

Every major prediction platform does this. Polymarket shows you the market price. Kalshi shows you the order book. Metaculus shows you the community median. Manifold shows you the odds. They all display the aggregate before you commit your own judgment, and then they're surprised when the outputs aren't as accurate as advertised.

But accuracy isn't even the biggest problem. The biggest problem is that prediction markets have become gambling platforms with a veneer of intellectual respectability.

Polymarket did $3.5 billion in volume in 2024. Insider trading allegations are stacking up — from bets on geopolitical events in Iran to weather patterns in Paris. CNN is investigating "death markets." Congress has declared the status quo unsustainable. Romania blocked 300 prediction market sites. Google now only allows ads for CFTC-regulated platforms. FanDuel, DraftKings, and Crypto.com are all racing to get licensed as gambling operators.

The entire industry is converging on a single model: regulated betting on future events dressed up as "forecasting."

## How It Works: The Blind Consensus Protocol

The Blind Consensus Protocol is inspired by the Delphi method, a structured forecasting technique developed by the RAND Corporation in the 1950s. It's been modernized with proper scoring rules and digital infrastructure, but the core insight is the same: **the best forecast comes from independent judgment, then structured revision.**

Every question on Baycast goes through two phases:

### Phase A — The Blind Phase

When a new question goes live, all forecasters submit their probability estimates *without seeing what anyone else thinks*. No market price. No community median. No anchors. Just you, the question, the resolution criteria, and a probability slider.

Your forecast is recorded with a timestamp and locked in. This phase captures the pure, independent signal from every participant — the exact thing that makes collective intelligence work. A doctor's healthcare insight. A trader's market intuition. A scientist's domain knowledge. All preserved, uncorrupted by groupthink.

### Phase B — The Revision Phase

After the blind phase closes (or after a minimum number of forecasts are submitted), all predictions are revealed. Participants can see the aggregate and each other's individual forecasts.

Now, structured revision begins. Each forecaster can update their prediction *once*. Not unlimited trading. Not real-time price movement. One deliberate revision based on new information from the group.

This is where it gets interesting. If the aggregate shifts your view, update. If you think the crowd is wrong, stand your ground. But either way, your original blind prediction is preserved and scored independently. The revision is scored too. You get credit for both your raw judgment and your ability to learn from the group.

The result is something no prediction market can produce: a dataset that separates *independent judgment* from *information integration*. Two different skills. Both measurable. Both valuable.

### Scoring: Brier + Logarithmic

Every prediction is scored using the **Brier score** — a proper scoring rule where honesty is mathematically optimal. Your best strategy is always to report your true belief. Not to hedge. Not to game the system. Just be honest about what you think will happen.

This is fundamentally different from prediction markets, where your optimal strategy depends on the current price, your bankroll, and what you think others will do. In markets, the game is being right *relative to the price*. In Brier-scored polling, the game is being right *relative to reality*.

Baycast's leaderboards rank forecasters by **calibration** — how well their probabilities match real-world outcomes. A forecaster who consistently says "70%" for events that happen 70% of the time rises to the top. A gambler who got lucky on a few high-leverage bets doesn't.

We also use logarithmic scoring for categorical questions, ensuring that extreme confidence in the right direction is rewarded proportionally — and punished severely when wrong.
